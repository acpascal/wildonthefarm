## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

There are several "chamomiles," and it's worth telling them apart. The one that grows in our garden is **feverfew** (*Tanacetum parthenium*), often simply called *manzanilla* in Latin America. You can recognize it by its broad, flat, lobed leaves of a soft green, very different from the fennel-fine foliage of German chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*), the one in bedtime teas. Both belong to the daisy family, but they have neither the same composition nor the same uses. Feverfew owes its reputation to **parthenolide**, a bitter compound in its leaves. It has been used since antiquity for fever and headaches, and today it's studied mainly for **migraine prevention**: several clinical trials suggest a modest reduction in the frequency of attacks, but results remain uneven from one study to the next. The cautions matter: it is **not recommended during pregnancy** or breastfeeding, it can cause mouth ulcers when the fresh leaves are chewed and reactions in people allergic to the daisy family (ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold), and it may interact with blood thinners. After long-term use, it's better to stop gradually rather than abruptly.

## How to Use It

Feverfew isn't a kitchen herb: its leaves are frankly **bitter**, with a camphor-and-citrus scent. The simplest use is as a **tea** — a small spoonful of dried flowers and leaves in a cup of just-boiled water, steeped for five minutes, with a little honey or a few leaves of mint or verbena to soften the bitterness. The English tradition was to slip one or two fresh leaves into a buttered sandwich, precisely to mask the taste and avoid direct contact with the mouth. For keeping, cut the stems in full bloom and dry them in small bunches, upside down, in the shade and in the air. If you're after the gentle, apple-scented bedtime tea, German chamomile is the one you want: feverfew is a different plant, more bitter and more potent. In the garden, its sprays of little daisies last very well in a vase.

## How to Grow It Organically

It's one of the easiest medicinal plants: once settled, it looks after itself.

- **Sow on the surface.** The very fine seeds need light to germinate: set them on the compost without covering them, and they come up in one to two weeks.
- **Sun or part shade.** It flowers best in sun but is quite happy in dappled light.
- **Ordinary, well-drained soil.** It doesn't need rich earth; it only dislikes standing water.
- **Thirty centimeters between plants.** Each one forms a clump forty to sixty centimeters high.
- **Cut back after flowering.** A firm trim brings on fresh foliage and a new wave of flowers.
- **A short-lived perennial that reseeds.** Plants live two to three years, but self-sown seedlings take over; deadhead if you want to limit its spread.
- **Very few pests.** Its bitter, fragrant foliage is rarely attacked.

## Feverfew in Our Garden

Feverfew comes from the mountains of the Balkans and the Caucasus, and it naturalized long ago in the cooler regions of the Americas. At 1,800 meters, Horqueta suits it perfectly: with neither frost nor real heat, it flowers almost all year instead of for a single season. It has settled at the foot of the posts and along the edges of the beds, among the other plants, and reseeds itself from one season to the next — we simply move the young plants to where we'd like to see them bloom. In the rainy season, it's the excess moisture that wears it out: we open up the clumps by cutting them back after each flowering.

## Good Companions

- **Cabbages and other brassicas.** Its strong scent is said to keep aphids and other unwanted insects away.
- **Roses.** A classic cottage-garden pairing, for the same reason.
- **Mint, verbena, and thyme.** Neighbors from the tea corner, with similar needs.
- **The edges of paths and beds.** There it forms a flowering border that reseeds without getting in the way of the crops.

## Plants to Avoid

- **Crops that depend on pollinators (zucchini, cucumber, strawberries).** The scent of feverfew is said to displease bees: better to keep it a few meters away.
- **Delicate young seedlings.** It reseeds abundantly and can quickly smother them.
- **Plants that like wet soil.** It doesn't cope well with being watered as much as they are.

## Good to Know

- **Its name comes from the Latin *febrifugia* — "that which drives out fever."** It's one of its oldest uses.
- **A legend ties it to the Parthenon.** *Parthenium* is said to recall that it was used to treat a workman who fell from the temple during its construction.
- **It isn't the chamomile of bedtime teas.** To tell them apart, look at the leaves: broad and lobed for feverfew, fine as threads for German chamomile.
- **It's a cousin of tansy and chrysanthemums.** For a long time it was in fact classified among the chrysanthemums.
- **It has double-flowered varieties.** The white pompom forms are grown as ornamentals.
