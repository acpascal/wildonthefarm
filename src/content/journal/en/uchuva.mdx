## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

Uchuva (*Physalis peruviana*) has almost as many names as countries where it grows: uchuva in Colombia, aguaymanto in Peru, uvilla in Ecuador, physalis, goldenberry, Cape gooseberry, or, in France, **amour en cage** — "love in a cage" — after the calyx that wraps the fruit like a little lantern. This orange berry, the size of a large cherry, is rich in **vitamin C** and in **provitamin A** in the form of carotenoids, and also provides B vitamins, iron, phosphorus, and fiber. It contains compounds specific to the genus *Physalis* as well — **withanolides** and physalins — studied in the lab for their anti-inflammatory properties; promising research, but still a long way from confirmed applications in humans. One important caution: like many nightshades, the plant contains alkaloids in its **leaves, stems, and unripe green fruit**, which must not be eaten. Only fully ripe fruit — golden orange, with a dry, papery calyx — is edible.

## How to Eat It

Uchuva is eaten above all **fresh**, slipped out of its lantern at the last moment. Its flavor is a surprise: tart and sweet at once, with notes that recall cherry tomato, pineapple, and mango. Eat it as it is, add it to a fruit salad, or use it to decorate desserts, the calyx folded back like petals above the fruit — which is how you often see it in European pastry shops. In the Andes, it's readily turned into **jams**, syrups, and sweet-and-sour sauces served with grilled meats; in Peru, aguaymanto is also enjoyed dried, like a tart raisin. It pairs beautifully with dark chocolate — dipped in melted chocolate while you hold it by the calyx, it becomes a spectacular treat — and brings a lovely acidity to savory salads, with arugula and fresh cheese. Kept in its calyx, it stores for several weeks somewhere cool.

## How to Grow It Organically

Uchuva is a generous, undemanding perennial that can produce for several years — its main requests are space and some support.

- **Sow in a nursery.** The tiny seeds germinate in two to three weeks in warm seed compost; transplant the seedlings when they reach about fifteen centimeters. Stem cuttings work very well too.
- **A vigorous bush that needs staking.** The plant can reach a meter to a meter and a half high and as wide; a stake or light trellis keeps fruit-laden branches from collapsing onto the ground.
- **Well-drained soil, not too rich.** Soil too rich in nitrogen gives lush foliage at the expense of fruit; a moderate helping of compost is enough.
- **A harvest spread over time.** The first fruit appears four to five months after planting, then the plant flowers and fruits continuously. Harvest when the calyx is dry and straw-colored — ripe fruit often drops to the ground by itself.
- **Maintenance pruning** after the big harvests, to open up the plant and encourage new fruiting shoots.
- **Few serious pests**, apart from the odd caterpillar or stink bug; in very humid climates, good air circulation limits fungal diseases on the dense foliage.

## Uchuva in Our Garden

Uchuva doesn't need acclimatizing to Horqueta: here it finds, almost identically, the conditions of its native mountains. *Physalis peruviana* grows naturally in the Andes, generally between 1,500 and 3,000 meters above sea level, in cool, humid, frost-free climates — exactly the profile of Boquete's cloud forest at 1,800 meters. Colombia, the world's leading producer, grows it precisely in its highlands. On the farm it's an almost self-sufficient crop: once settled on a sunny terrace, it produces for months without asking for much more than a stake and regular picking. It also tends to reseed itself from fruit that falls to the ground — young plants often appear by themselves around the mother plant, easy to transplant elsewhere.

## Good Companions

- **Low aromatic herbs (basil, parsley).** They enjoy the light shade of the bush and attract beneficial insects.
- **Nectar-rich flowers (marigold, borage).** They attract pollinators, useful for fruit set, even though uchuva is largely self-fertile.
- **Legumes as the previous crop**, which enrich the soil with nitrogen in moderation, without excess.

## Plants to Avoid

- **Other nightshades right next to it (tomato, potato, eggplant, tree tomato, naranjilla).** They share the same diseases and pests; it's better to space them out and alternate them in rotation.
- **Fennel**, for its general allelopathic effect.
- **Low crops that need full sun**, which the dense foliage of a mature uchuva could shade.

## Good to Know

- **In France it's called "love in a cage."** A poetic name for a fruit enclosed in its calyx as if in a little paper cage.
- **It's in the same family as the tomato.** Like tomato, potato, and naranjilla, uchuva is a nightshade — its closest cousin is the Mexican tomatillo, used in salsa verde.
- **Its calyx protects it for weeks.** Kept in its lantern, the berry stores far longer than once it's been husked.
- **It travelled the world in the 19th century.** Introduced to South Africa, where it's known as the Cape gooseberry, then to Australia and India, it's now grown on almost every continent.
