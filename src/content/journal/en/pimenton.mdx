## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

In Panama, *pimentón* means the sweet pepper (*Capsicum annuum*), the heatless cousin of the chilies. It's one of the vegetables richest in **vitamin C**: weight for weight, a red pepper contains markedly more than an orange. It also provides **provitamin A** in the form of carotenoids, vitamin B6, folate, and vitamin E, for very few calories. Color matters: a green pepper is simply a pepper picked before it's ripe, and it's as it reddens that it builds up vitamin C, sugars, and antioxidant pigments such as **capsanthin**, which gives it its red. Unlike chilies, it contains practically no capsaicin, the molecule behind the heat. Its fiber-rich skin can be hard to digest for sensitive stomachs: roasting and peeling it solves that. As with all nightshades, only the fruit is eaten — the leaves and stems are not edible.

## How to Eat It

Pimentón is one of the pillars of Panamanian cooking: finely chopped with onion, garlic, and culantro, it makes the **sofrito**, the fragrant base of rice dishes, beans, *guisos*, and soups. Raw, in strips or diced, it brings crunch and freshness to salads and ceviche. **Roasted** over a flame or in the oven until the skin blackens, then peeled, it turns silky and sweet: serve it with olive oil and garlic, or blend it into a sauce or soup. Small fruits like ours lend themselves to being stuffed whole or pickled in vinegar. Brief cooking better preserves the vitamin C, which prolonged heat partly destroys. It keeps for a week in the refrigerator and freezes very well, cut into strips, with no need to blanch.

## How to Grow It Organically

In the tropics, pimentón isn't the annual of European vegetable gardens: with no frost, it lives for several years.

- **Sow in a nursery.** The seeds come up in one to two weeks in warm seed compost; transplant the seedlings at about fifteen centimeters.
- **The warmest, sunniest spot.** It's a heat-loving plant: sheltered from wind, against a wall or a sun-facing slope.
- **Rich, loose, well-drained soil.** Mature compost at planting, without excess nitrogen, which would give leaves at the expense of fruit.
- **Regular watering and a good mulch.** Irregular watering causes brown patches at the tip of the fruit.
- **A stake.** The brittle branches bend under the weight of the fruit.
- **A light pruning** after each big harvest, to open up the plant and bring on new shoots.
- **Harvest green or red.** Picked green, the fruit is firmer and slightly bitter; left on the plant, it reddens and sweetens.
- **Against aphids**, encourage beneficial insects with flowers nearby, and avoid growing nightshades in the same place year after year.

## Pimentón in Our Garden

Pimentón is a plant of warmth, and Horqueta, at 1,800 meters, mostly offers it coolness: it grows more slowly here than in the lowlands, and its fruit stays small. But the absence of frost changes everything — instead of dying at the end of the season, the plant keeps growing year after year. The one in the photo, settled near the green-roofed lodges and leaning on a bamboo stake, has become a real shrub with woody branches, carrying green and red fruit at the same time. We pick them as the kitchen needs them, and an occasional pruning is enough to keep it in shape.

## Good Companions

- **Basil and parsley.** They enjoy its light shade and attract beneficial insects.
- **Marigold and nasturtium.** They bring in pollinators and draw aphids away.
- **Onion, garlic, and chives.** Their scent is said to discourage aphids.
- **Carrot and lettuce.** Low crops that fill the ground at its foot without competing with it.

## Plants to Avoid

- **Other nightshades right next to it (tomato, potato, eggplant, tree tomato, naranjilla).** They share the same diseases and pests.
- **Fennel**, for its general allelopathic effect.
- **Tall crops that would shade it**, since it needs all the warmth and light available.

## Good to Know

- **One word, two meanings.** In Spain, *pimentón* is the powder of dried peppers — paprika; in Panama, Colombia, and Venezuela, it's the fresh sweet pepper.
- **All peppers come from the Americas.** Unknown in Europe before Columbus's voyage, they were brought back at the end of the 15th century.
- **Green pepper isn't a variety.** It's a red pepper that hasn't finished ripening.
- **Its sweetness comes down to a single gene.** In sweet peppers, the gene that drives the production of capsaicin is inactive.
- **In the tropics, it's a perennial.** Without frost, a plant can live and produce for several years, and its wood hardens like a shrub's.
