## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

The tree tomato (*Solanum betaceum*, formerly classified as *Cyphomandra betacea*) is a remarkably nutrient-dense fruit: it's rich in **vitamin C**, in **provitamin A** in the form of carotenoids, and in vitamin E — a combination of antioxidants that's fairly rare in a single fruit. Red- or purple-fleshed varieties also contain anthocyanins, the same antioxidant pigments found in dark berries, while yellow or orange varieties are slightly sweeter and less tart. It also provides a good amount of iron and potassium, as well as worthwhile fiber concentrated mainly in its pulp and small edible seeds. Its trade name, "tamarillo," isn't an old botanical term but a marketing invention of New Zealand growers in 1967, who were looking for a distinctive name to export it without confusing it with the tomato — throughout its South American home region, the fruit is still called "tomate de árbol," a name that describes its true nature much more simply: a fruit of the tomato family, but growing on a shrub.

## How to Eat It

The tree tomato is rarely eaten as is, skin and all: its smooth but rather bitter skin comes off easily if you blanch the fruit for a few seconds, a bit like a regular tomato. Its pulp — tart and slightly sweet, with a texture reminiscent of a cross between tomato and passion fruit — is enjoyed plain with a spoon, as juice (often mixed with other fruits to soften its acidity), or made into jam. In Ecuador, where it holds a place of honor in everyday cooking, it's best known as *ají de tomate de árbol*, a spicy sauce with chili, onion, and cilantro that accompanies most traditional dishes — a savory rather than sweet use, quite far from what its name might suggest. It also takes very well to brief cooking into a lightly sweetened compote, served at breakfast with fresh cheese — another habit deeply rooted in the Ecuadorian and Colombian Andes.

## How to Grow It Organically

The tree tomato is a relatively easy crop once established, but it takes patience: unlike the regular tomato, it's a small perennial shrub that takes many months before its first harvest, and several years to reach full yield.

- **Sow in a nursery or grow from cuttings** — both methods are common; seed gives a more vigorous, better-rooted shrub in the long run, while cuttings bear fruit sooner and preserve the exact characteristics of the mother plant.
- **A cool, stable, frost-free climate.** It's native to the high Andean valleys and poorly suited to sustained tropical heat and large temperature swings — a fundamental difference from the regular tomato, which craves heat.
- **Rich, deep, well-drained soil**, similar to what the ordinary tomato likes, with regular additions of compost to support the growth of a shrub that produces over several years rather than a single season.
- **Slow growth at first, then a harvest spread over several years.** The first fruit usually appears 18 months to 2 years after sowing, but once established the shrub goes on producing for 5 to 10 years with minimal care.
- **Light pruning to open up the canopy**, useful for limiting fungal diseases in a humid climate and for making harvesting easier, since the shrub can reach 2 to 3 meters if left unpruned.
- **Few serious pests at cool altitudes**, its main weak point being fruit flies, against which regularly gathering fallen fruit remains the best organic prevention.

## Tree Tomato in Our Garden

Unlike zucchini or the regular tomato, the tree tomato doesn't need to be "adapted" to Horqueta's cool climate — it's at home here. *Solanum betaceum* is native to the Andean valleys of Ecuador, Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia, at altitudes that overlap almost exactly with those of the Boquete area, with a cool, stable climate all year round, far from the extremes of tropical heat. It's no coincidence that tomate de árbol is now grown as an alternative crop in the Chiriquí highlands — the same region as Horqueta — where local agricultural studies identify it as one of the high-potential fruits of the Cerro Punta uplands. The volcanic soils rich in organic matter, so similar to those of the Andean foothills it comes from, make this shrub an almost obvious choice for a cloud forest garden — a plant that, here, isn't a gamble but a matter of geography.

## Good Companions

- **Basil and other aromatic herbs**, planted at the foot of the shrub, which enjoy the light shade of its canopy while repelling certain insects attracted to nightshade foliage.
- **Ground-covering legumes**, such as bush beans, which enrich the soil with nitrogen around the shrub without competing with its deeper root system.
- **Nasturtium**, useful as an aphid trap — a pest that readily attacks the tender young foliage of nightshades in general.
- **Nectar-rich flowers** (borage, cosmos), which attract the pollinators needed for good fruit set; the tree tomato is partly self-fertile but produces more with active cross-pollination.

## Plants to Avoid

- **Potato and the regular tomato**, close cousins in the nightshade family, with which the tree tomato shares several fungal diseases and the risk of passing on soil nematodes — a caution that matters all the more because the shrub stays in place for several years and so doesn't benefit from normal crop rotation.
- **Very thirsty crops planted in direct root competition**, which can deprive the young shrub of the steady moisture it needs during its first two years of establishment, before its root system is fully developed.

## Good to Know

- **Its old scientific name, *Cyphomandra betacea*, is still sometimes used.** Its classification was revised to place it back in the large genus *Solanum*, which also includes tomato, potato, and eggplant — a single botanical family with astonishingly varied culinary uses.
- **The name "tamarillo" didn't exist before 1967.** It was coined by New Zealand growers, who had been growing the plant since the early 20th century as "tree tomato," precisely to distance it from that overly direct association with the tomato when marketing it for export.
- **It grows as well in a pot or small space as in open ground**, its modest shrub size (rarely more than 3 meters) making it an attractive option for a small garden, unlike most fruit trees.
- **Its color doesn't tell you everything about its taste.** Contrary to popular belief, red fruits aren't always the tartest: the less colorful yellow and orange varieties are generally the sweetest and least acidic — a useful detail when choosing a variety to plant.
