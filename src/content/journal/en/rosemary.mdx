## Medicinal & Therapeutic Properties

Rosemary has one of the longest folk-medicine pedigrees of any Mediterranean herb, historically nicknamed "the herb of remembrance." In traditional and modern herbal practice, it's commonly used as a morning tonic — taken as a light infusion to support mental clarity, focus, and memory, which is exactly why it earns its place alongside jiaogulan and nettle in our morning tea blend. Herbalists have long valued rosemary as a mild circulatory stimulant, traditionally used to encourage blood flow toward the head and extremities, and its slightly bitter, aromatic character has made it a traditional digestive "bitter," commonly used to support healthy liver function and ease sluggish digestion after heavy meals. It's also prized in folk tradition as a gentle, non-caffeinated pick-me-up and general tonic during periods of fatigue or mental strain, and its aromatic oils are noted in herbal literature for their antioxidant richness — one reason rosemary shows up as often in traditional skin and hair care as it does in the teacup.

## Culinary Interest

Rosemary is one of the most recognizable culinary herbs of Mediterranean cooking, prized for its resinous, pine-and-citrus aroma and warm, slightly bitter bite. Fresh sprigs are milder and a touch sweeter than dried rosemary. Its sturdy needle-like leaves hold up beautifully to high heat and long cooking, making it a natural match for roasted meats, roasted root vegetables, and hearty breads. Whole sprigs are often tossed onto coals or steeped into olive oil, and it pairs naturally with garlic, lemon, and thyme. Beyond savory cooking, rosemary makes a fragrant, invigorating herbal tea on its own — a simple hot-water infusion is enough to draw out its full flavor, which is why it anchors our morning blend alongside jiaogulan and nettle.

## How To Grow It

Rosemary is a Mediterranean shrub adapted to hot sun, poor rocky soil, and dry air — so it faces real challenges in our cool, misty, humid cloud forest at 1,800m. Constantly damp soil and lingering humidity around the foliage are its main enemies, commonly leading to root rot rather than lush growth. That said, it can still be grown successfully with the right setup: we choose the sunniest, most open and breeze-exposed spot on the property, and plant in raised beds filled with a fast-draining mix of sand, gravel, and compost rather than heavy garden soil. We water only once the soil has dried out, and prune regularly to keep the shrub open and airy. Propagation is best done from stem cuttings rather than seed. Harvest young, tender shoots as needed year-round, cutting lightly rather than into old woody growth.
