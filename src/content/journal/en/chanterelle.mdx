## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

The chanterelle is neither a plant nor a vegetable: it's a fungus of the genus *Cantharellus*, of which we see only the "fruit," the rest living underground, entwined with the roots of trees. Very low in calories, it provides **fiber**, **potassium**, copper, iron, and B vitamins, **vitamin B3** in particular. Above all, it's one of the few foods naturally rich in **vitamin D**, which the mushroom makes in the light, rather as our skin does. Its orange-yellow color comes from **carotenoids**, the same pigments as in carrots. One standard caution: like most wild mushrooms, the chanterelle is **always eaten cooked** — raw, it's indigestible and can cause digestive upset.

**And one rule that comes before all the others: never eat a wild mushroom you haven't identified with certainty, ideally with someone who knows the species of the region.** Some orange mushrooms that resemble it are toxic. This article tells the story of our foraging; it is no substitute for an identification guide.

## How to Eat It

The chanterelle has firm flesh, a delicate, slightly peppery flavor, and that scent of apricot that makes it unique. Don't wash it under running water — it would soak it up: brush it and scrape the stem with the tip of a knife. In the kitchen, the step that changes everything is to **dry-sauté** it first in a hot pan, so it gives up its water, before adding butter, shallot or garlic, and then parsley at the last moment. Prepared like this, it needs nothing more than a slice of toast, and goes wonderfully with an **omelet**, scrambled eggs, roast chicken, fresh pasta, or a risotto; a spoonful of cream turns it into a sauce. It dries less well than other mushrooms, turning leathery: to keep it, better to sauté and then freeze it, or preserve it in vinegar.

## How to Recognize and Pick It

The chanterelle can't be cultivated: it lives in association with the roots of certain trees, and no one has yet managed to produce it like a button mushroom. The whole art, then, lies in recognizing it.

- **Ridges, not gills.** Under the cap, the chanterelle has no true gills, thin and crowded, but **thick ridges**, blunt-edged, that fork and run down the stem. This is the most important feature.
- **A funnel shape.** The cap, domed at first, hollows out with age, with a wavy, irregular edge.
- **White, stringy flesh.** The stem tears into strands, like string cheese; the inside is pale, not orange.
- **A scent of apricot.** Held to the nose, a fresh chanterelle smells of ripe fruit.
- **It grows on the ground, never on wood.** Alone or in small scattered groups among moss and leaves, at the foot of trees — not in tight clusters on a stump.
- **Pick cleanly.** Cut or gently detach the mushroom, clean it on the spot, carry it in a basket rather than a plastic bag, and leave the smallest ones and part of every patch.

## Chanterelles in the Cloud Forest

We associate chanterelles with the forests of Europe and North America, but the genus *Cantharellus* is also present in the mountains of Central America, where several species live in symbiosis with the **oaks** of the highland forests. Horqueta's cloud forest — cool, mossy, and watered for a large part of the year — gives them exactly the moisture they look for. We find them in the forest around the farm during the rainy season, a few days after good downpours, often in the same spots from one year to the next: the mushroom we pick is only the fruit of an underground organism that has lived there for a long time. It's foraging on a walk, never a harvest: a few handfuls are enough for the kitchen, and the forest keeps the rest.

## Its Tree Companions

- **Oaks.** The chanterelle is a mycorrhizal fungus: its filaments wrap the tree's roots, bring it water and minerals, and receive sugars in return. No tree, no chanterelle.
- **Moss and leaf litter.** They hold at ground level the constant moisture the fungus needs in order to fruit.
- **An old, undisturbed forest.** This partnership takes years to establish; it disappears when trees are cut or the soil is compacted.

## Lookalikes to Avoid

- **Orange mushrooms growing in clusters on wood.** *Omphalotus* species, known as jack-o'-lanterns, have true thin gills and grow in tight bunches on stumps; they are **toxic** and cause violent digestive upset.
- **The false chanterelle (*Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca*).** More orange, softer, with true gills, thin and crowded; of little interest and sometimes indigestible.
- **Any mushroom where a single feature doesn't match.** When in doubt, don't eat it — and never rely on a photo or an app to decide.

## Good to Know

- **Its French names are *girolle* and *chanterelle*.** "Chanterelle" comes from the Greek *kantharos*, a cup, for its shape; the Germans call it *Pfifferling*, for its peppery edge.
- **It's rarely wormy.** Insects leave it alone, which makes it one of the cleanest mushrooms to pick.
- **"The" chanterelle is really a large family.** Genetic studies have shown that the chanterelles of the Americas are not the same species as Europe's, but close cousins.
- **It grows slowly.** A chanterelle can take several weeks to reach its size, where other mushrooms come up overnight.
- **It makes vitamin D in the sun.** Like our skin, the mushroom produces it under the effect of light.
