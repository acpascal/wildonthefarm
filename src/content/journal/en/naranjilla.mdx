## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

Naranjilla (*Solanum quitoense*), also called lulo in Colombia, is a fruit remarkably rich in **vitamin C** — often comparable to, or even higher than, classic citrus fruits weight for weight — which makes it a natural ally against minor winter infections in the regions where it grows. Its green, tart pulp, surrounding small edible seeds, also contains a good dose of **potassium**, iron, and provitamin A, as well as antioxidant phenolic compounds concentrated mostly in the skin — hence the interest, when possible, in juicing the whole fruit rather than peeling it completely. Its botanical name, *quitoense*, pays direct tribute to Quito, the capital of Ecuador, so closely is the plant associated with its country of origin; "naranjilla" literally means "little orange" in Spanish, after the orange color of its fuzzy skin — which, once peeled, has nothing to do with the flavor of the citrus its name brings to mind. Finally, it's a low-calorie fruit whose pronounced acidity makes it more an ingredient for drinks or desserts than a fruit you bite into as is.

## How to Eat It

Naranjilla is almost always eaten processed rather than raw as is, its skin being bitter and its pulp very acidic on its own. Cut in half and squeezed, or blended then strained, it yields a pale green juice with a surprising fragrance, often described as a blend of pineapple, rhubarb, and a hint of citrus — Ecuador's unofficial national drink, served sweetened and sometimes diluted with water or milk. In Colombia, it's the base of *lulada*, a refreshing drink from the Cali region that combines the fruit's pulp with crushed ice and sometimes a little sugar, served in the street as well as at home. It also works very well in sorbets and ice creams, its strong acidity balancing naturally with sugar and fat, and in jams and savory sauces to go with grilled meat or fish — a lesser-known use that's much appreciated in some Andean regions.

## How to Grow It Organically

Naranjilla is a generous plant in a climate that suits it, but it has a reputation for being fussy outside its natural habitat — sensitive to heat and soil pests, and equipped with foliage covered in small spines that make it awkward to handle.

- **Sow in a nursery; germination can be slow and patchy.** Seeds generally take 15 to 30 days to germinate, and light shade at the seedling stage encourages stronger rooting before planting out.
- **A plant of cool, humid climates, poorly suited to sustained tropical heat.** It's native to the high Andean valleys of Ecuador, Colombia, and northern Peru and quickly suffers beyond a certain threshold of prolonged heat — the exact opposite, on this point, of zucchini or chili.
- **Rich soil that's cool and constantly moist, without standing water.** Naranjilla has a shallow root system and copes poorly with dry spells, even short ones, which quickly affect the quality of its fruit.
- **A plant covered in fine spines on its stems and the undersides of its leaves** — something to know before you start: gloves are recommended for care and harvesting, and selected so-called "spineless" varieties are still a minority.
- **Production begins 8 to 12 months after sowing**, faster than tree tomato, but with a shorter productive life (usually 2 to 4 years) — a plant to renew more regularly in the garden.
- **Particular vigilance against nematodes and fruit flies**, its two main enemies in organic growing; good rotation and repellent plants around the base remain the best prevention available without chemical inputs.

## Naranjilla in Our Garden

Like the tree tomato, naranjilla doesn't need to be forced to adapt to Horqueta's climate — here it finds, almost identically, the conditions of its native habitat. *Solanum quitoense* grows naturally in the cool, humid valleys of the Ecuadorian and Colombian Andes, generally between 1,000 and 2,300 meters above sea level, a range that overlaps directly with the Boquete area. The cloud forest, with its almost constant atmospheric humidity and stable year-round temperatures, matches almost exactly the microclimate in which this plant evolved in the wild before being domesticated in Andean gardens. Agricultural studies carried out in the nearby highlands of Cerro Punta, in the same province of Chiriquí, also confirm naranjilla's potential as a fruit crop suited to these cool Panamanian altitudes — a fruit that, here, isn't exotic in the sense of needing to be pampered, but truly at home.

## Good Companions

- **Trees or large shrubs providing light shade**, since naranjilla grows naturally in the understory or along forest edges in its native habitat, where it enjoys filtered light rather than constant full sun.
- **Low-competition ground covers**, which help keep the constant moisture this plant needs around its shallow roots, without competing with it for water.
- **Nematode-repelling plants**, such as certain varieties of marigold (tagetes), planted along the edges to limit one of naranjilla's main underground pests.
- **Other cool-climate Andean fruit crops** (tree tomato, strawberry), which share similar needs for coolness and moisture and make it possible to group the garden's watering zones sensibly.

## Plants to Avoid

- **Other nightshades grown right next to it** (tomato, potato, tree tomato), with which naranjilla shares susceptibility to the same fungal diseases and the same soil nematodes — a matter of rotation caution more than strict incompatibility.
- **Full-sun spots with no shade at all**, which aren't strictly a "plant to avoid" but a location to avoid: overly intense exposure stresses the plant and markedly reduces the quality of its harvest in an already warm climate.
- **Soils that dry out quickly**, such as unmulched sandy slopes, where naranjilla struggles to maintain the constant moisture its shallow roots need — although this point has more to do with the soil than with any particular neighboring plant.

## Good to Know

- **Naranjilla long resisted every attempt at commercial cultivation outside the Andes.** Its susceptibility to nematodes and diseases outside its native climate long limited its cultivation to Ecuador and Colombia, where even today it remains largely a regional crop rather than a globalized export like bananas or pineapples.
- **Its name says orange; its inside says green.** The contrast between its fuzzy orange skin, reminiscent of a small orange (hence "naranjilla"), and its bright green pulp often surprises those discovering it for the first time.
- **It belongs to the same family as potato, tomato, and eggplant.** The genus *Solanum* is one of the largest in the plant kingdom, with more than 1,000 species, only a handful of which are grown for human food.
- **In Colombia, almost no one says "naranjilla."** The name "lulo," probably of Indigenous origin, is by far the most common on the Colombian side, while "naranjilla" remains the dominant term in Ecuador — two names for one and the same fruit, depending on which side of the Andean border you're on.
