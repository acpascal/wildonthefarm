## Nutritional Properties & Benefits

Purslane (*Portulaca oleracea*) holds a unique place among leafy greens: it's one of the few notable plant sources of **omega-3 fatty acids** (in the form of alpha-linolenic acid), usually associated mainly with oily fish and flaxseed. Studies have measured particularly high omega-3 levels for a leafy green, which has earned it renewed interest in nutrition circles in recent years. It's also rich in **vitamin C**, in **vitamin A** in the form of beta-carotene, in magnesium and potassium, and contains specific antioxidants such as melatonin and various flavonoid compounds. Like chard and beetroot, it contains oxalates, something to keep in mind for people prone to kidney stones. Its fleshy, slightly mucilaginous texture, similar to that of some succulents, comes from its ability to store water in its leaves — an adaptation that lets it survive drought better than most leafy greens.

## How to Eat It

Purslane can be eaten raw or cooked, with a texture and flavor that change noticeably depending on how it's prepared. **Raw**, in salads, its leaves and fleshy young stems bring a juicy crunch and a slight lemony tang, a bit like a cross between spinach and lamb's lettuce — excellent with a simple vinaigrette, feta, or tomatoes, with which it forms a classic pairing in several Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cuisines. **Cooked**, quickly sautéed with garlic and olive oil, it loses some of its crunch but develops an almost mucilaginous texture, comparable to okra, which makes it a good natural thickener for soups and stews — it's in fact a traditional ingredient in some Greek and Mexican dishes (*verdolagas*), often simmered with tomato, onion, and sometimes pork or chicken. Its thicker stems also pickle very well, a little like gherkins.

## How to Grow It Organically

Purslane is probably the most forgiving vegetable on this list — so much so that the difficulty usually isn't getting it to grow, but rather stopping it from reseeding itself everywhere once it's settled in.

- **Sow on the surface, without burying the seeds.** Purslane's tiny seeds need light to germinate and should only be pressed very lightly onto the soil surface, never covered deeply. Germination is quick in warm weather, within a few days to a week.
- **A crop that loves heat and full sun**, unlike most of the leafy greens on this list — it's a plant of desert and Mediterranean origin, perfectly at ease with high temperatures that would make lettuce bolt.
- **Very little watering needed.** Its ability to store water in its fleshy leaves makes it one of the most drought-tolerant vegetables in the garden — occasional watering is plenty once the plant is established.
- **No particular soil requirements.** Purslane grows easily in poor, sandy, or compacted soils where few other vegetables would thrive — soil too rich in nitrogen sometimes even produces less flavorful foliage.
- **Harvest by pinching the stems.** Cut the stems about 5 cm above the ground; the plant regrows vigorously from the base, allowing several successive harvests from the same planting.
- **Keep an eye on self-seeding.** It reseeds very easily once it goes to seed, and in fact readily grows wild in many gardens without ever having been sown — worth knowing before you let it settle into a bed you later want to use for something else.

## Purslane in Our Garden

Unlike most of the leafy greens on this list, purslane here benefits less from the coolness of altitude than from exposure: it's in the warmest, sunniest corners of the garden, in poor, well-drained soil, that it's happiest and grows most vigorously. In a cloud forest where ambient humidity often stays high, it's also one of the few vegetables in the garden that copes without trouble with soil that dries out temporarily between waterings — an ideal companion crop for spots that are harder to irrigate regularly, or along the edge of a terrace in full sun.

## Good Companions

- **Corn.** A well-documented traditional pairing: purslane, as a creeping ground cover, holds moisture and cools the soil around the corn stalks, while itself enjoying the light shade cast by the tall stems.
- **Cucurbits (squash, melons, cucumbers).** Purslane makes an excellent living ground cover between the plants, limiting weeds and evaporation without ever competing with these deeper-rooted crops.
- **Tomatoes and sweet peppers.** Good general neighbors; purslane's low, creeping foliage doesn't shade the taller plants and helps keep the soil cool at their base.
- **Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli).** Some gardeners report that purslane, with its deep roots despite its creeping habit, helps bring up deep nutrients that benefit cabbages, although this effect remains mostly anecdotal.

## Plants to Avoid

Purslane has practically no documented incompatibility with other garden vegetables — one of the reasons it's often recommended as a versatile companion crop. The only real point of vigilance concerns its own behavior rather than any neighboring plant:

- **Don't let it go to seed near beds you want to keep "clean."** Its ability to reseed abundantly can make it invasive in neighboring crops if the stems aren't cut before the seeds form, especially near low-density crops such as squash or tomatoes.
- **A caution shared with ornamental moss rose (*Portulaca*)**, a decorative cousin sometimes planted in the same spot: the two can hybridize freely if their flowers are left to cross-pollinate, which doesn't affect edibility but can make the taste and appearance of the vegetable variety drift over successive self-sown generations.

## Good to Know

- **It's one of the oldest cultivated plants in the world.** Traces of its consumption have been found at archaeological sites several thousand years old, and the plant now grows wild on almost every continent, making it one of the most widespread edible plants on the planet.
- **Long considered a mere weed in North America and Europe**, it has enjoyed renewed culinary interest over the past few decades, driven notably by its remarkable nutritional profile and by Mediterranean and Mexican cooking, where in reality it never stopped being appreciated.
- **It's one of the few known plants to combine two photosynthetic strategies.** Purslane normally runs on C4 photosynthesis, which is already water-efficient, but during prolonged drought it can also temporarily switch to a metabolism close to CAM — the one used by cacti and many succulents — before returning to its usual mode once watered. This dual strategy, still being studied by botanists, goes a long way toward explaining its astonishing drought tolerance.
- **There's an ornamental version with colorful foliage and flowers** (often sold as moss rose or *Portulaca grandiflora*), distinct from the vegetable version but also edible, although grown mainly for ornament rather than for the table.
