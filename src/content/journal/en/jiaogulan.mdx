## Medicinal & Therapeutic Properties

Jiaogulan has been used for centuries in the folk medicine of southern China, where mountain communities drank it as a daily tonic and associated it with stamina and long life — hence nicknames like "immortality herb" and "southern ginseng." Herbalists classify it as a true adaptogen — one of the more clearly recognized examples of the category, alongside ginseng and rhodiola — meaning it's traditionally understood to help the body adapt to physical and mental stress rather than stimulate it outright. Its leaves contain gypenosides, compounds structurally similar to (and more numerous than) the ginsenosides found in ginseng, which is why the two plants are often compared. In traditional and modern herbal practice, jiaogulan is commonly used to support resilience to fatigue and stress and as part of general wellness routines, with some traditional and preliminary research also associating it with support for healthy circulation, healthy cholesterol levels, and antioxidant activity. Because of its effect on circulation, it's traditionally advised that anyone on blood-thinning medication check with their doctor before regular use.

## Culinary Interest

Jiaogulan's most popular everyday use is as a brewed tea, which is why it's sometimes called "sweet tea vine" throughout Asia. Fresh or dried leaves are steeped in hot water for a few minutes, and the leaves can typically be re-infused two or three times before losing flavor. The taste is often described as mildly sweet and grassy, with a vegetal, faintly cucumber-like note (a nod to its place in the gourd family) balanced by a light bitterness and a subtle licorice-like finish. It's the base of our morning tea blend, alongside nettle and rosemary, and can be customized with a touch of honey. Beyond the cup, young tender leaves are sometimes tossed into salads for a mild green flavor, though tea remains by far its most common culinary role.

## How To Grow It

Jiaogulan is a fast-growing, tendril-climbing vine in the cucumber and gourd family, native to shaded, humid mountain forests across East and Southeast Asia — conditions that closely resemble our own cloud forest. It prefers rich, evenly moist, well-drained soil that's slightly acidic, enriched with compost. Partial shade or filtered light suits it well, since strong, direct sun can scorch the leaves, while our cooler, cloudier setting can support more sun exposure. Because it climbs by curling tendrils, it needs a trellis or similar support. Propagation is easiest from stem cuttings rooted in water or by dividing an established clump. Boquete's cool, misty, high-humidity cloud forest climate at 1,800 metres closely mirrors jiaogulan's native mountain habitat, making it one of the most naturally well-suited plants we grow — it thrives here as a lasting perennial.
